Health literacy · Not a diagnosis

Fatty liver disease rarely hurts — until it's late.

Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD) — officially renamed MASLD in 2023 — is the most common chronic liver disease on Earth, affecting roughly 1 in 3 adults. Most people feel completely normal while fat, then inflammation, then scar tissue quietly builds up over years or decades. This page explains what to watch for at each stage — and why the only way to know for sure is a proper medical check-up.

Only blood tests, imaging and a clinician's assessment can confirm it.

What is NAFLD — and why is everyone now calling it MASLD?

The condition didn't change. The name did.

Your liver processes fat. When more than about 5% of the liver's weight is fat, doctors call it a "fatty liver" (hepatic steatosis).[5] If that fat is not caused by significant alcohol use, and the person has at least one metabolic risk factor — such as excess body weight, elevated blood sugar, high blood pressure or abnormal cholesterol — the diagnosis is fatty liver disease of metabolic origin.

For nearly 40 years that condition was called Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD). In June 2023, three major liver societies (AASLD, EASL and ALEH) published a multi-society Delphi consensus — 236 experts from 56 countries — that retired the old name.[1] The reasons: "non-alcoholic" defined the disease by what it wasn't, and both "non-alcoholic" and "fatty" were rated as stigmatising by a majority of the panel. The new name points at the actual driver: metabolic dysfunction.

If your lab result, ultrasound report or old chart says "NAFLD" or "NASH," that diagnosis is still valid — it's simply the older wording for the same disease.

How the disease progresses — stage by stage

Fatty liver is a spectrum, not a single event. Understanding where a stage sits tells you what symptoms to expect — and how reversible it still is.

F0
Stage 1 · Simple steatosis

Fat builds up — usually with zero symptoms

More than 5% of liver cells contain fat. The liver may be slightly enlarged, but it still works normally. Most people are discovered by accident: routine bloodwork shows mildly raised liver enzymes, or an ultrasound done for another reason shows a "bright" liver.

Reversibility: Highly reversible. Weight loss, diet and activity can clear the fat entirely.

▼
F0–F1
Stage 2 · MASH (inflammation begins)

The fat starts irritating and injuring liver cells

Roughly 20–30% of people with MASLD progress to MASH.[4] The liver becomes inflamed and liver cells begin to die. Symptoms are still often absent or vague — fatigue, a dull ache under the right ribs. On average, fibrosis advances about one stage every 14 years in simple MASLD but every 7 years once MASH is present.[4]

Reversibility: Still largely reversible with treatment and lifestyle change.

▼
F2–F3
Stage 3 · Fibrosis (scarring)

Scar tissue replaces healthy liver tissue

Persistent inflammation lays down collagen. The liver stiffens. At F2–F3 (moderate to advanced fibrosis) the liver may still function well enough that you feel fine — this is why fibrosis stage, not symptoms, is the strongest predictor of outcomes.[7] Around 10–25% of people with MASH progress to advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis.[10]

Reversibility: Partially reversible. This is the stage where approved MASH medications may be considered by a specialist.

▼
F4
Stage 4 · Cirrhosis

Widespread, permanent scarring — and now symptoms appear

The liver is nodular, shrunken and stiff. Blood flow through it is obstructed (portal hypertension). This is typically when unmistakable signs show up: jaundice, abdominal fluid, confusion, easy bleeding. Scarring to cirrhosis is usually slow — years to decades.[4] Average 10-year survival at F4 is around 51%, versus ~89–93% at F0–F2.[4]

Reversibility: Largely irreversible. Treatment focuses on stopping further damage, managing complications, screening for liver cancer, and — in decompensated cases — transplant evaluation.

▼
ESLD
Stage 5 · Complications

Liver failure and liver cancer

Decompensated cirrhosis brings liver failure. Fatty liver disease is also now the fastest-growing cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (liver cancer) worldwide.[11] Importantly, up to a quarter or more of fatty-liver-related liver cancers occur in people who never reached cirrhosis[11] — another reason not to wait for symptoms before getting checked.

Reversibility: Not reversible. Requires specialist care.

One important caveat: progression is not a one-way conveyor belt. Long-term biopsy studies show that a meaningful share of people stay stable for years, and some improve — while roughly 20% progress faster than average.[7] Where you land depends heavily on diabetes control, body weight, alcohol, genetics and how early you act. That variability is exactly why individual medical assessment matters more than any general statistic on this page.

Early signs: the phase where almost nothing happens

This is the most important section on the page — because the early stage is the most treatable, and the least noticeable.

⚠ The central fact about early fatty liver

Most people with early NAFLD/MASLD have no symptoms whatsoever. It can take years — often decades — for fat accumulation to change how the organ works, so the liver can sustain serious damage before you feel anything at all.[2][3] Many people are not diagnosed until the disease has already progressed to MASH, and many more are never diagnosed. Do not use the absence of symptoms as evidence that your liver is healthy.

Early-stage symptoms

Simple steatosis → early MASH · F0–F1

When symptoms do appear this early, they are nonspecific — meaning dozens of other conditions cause the same things. The two most commonly reported are fatigue and abdominal discomfort.[2]

  • Persistent fatigue or weakness — tiredness that doesn't resolve with rest, and is easy to blame on work, age or stress.
  • Discomfort, fullness or a dull ache in the upper right abdomen — under the right rib cage, where the liver sits. May feel sharp, dull or aching.
  • A generally "unwell" feeling (malaise) with no clear explanation.
  • Mildly elevated liver enzymes (ALT / AST) found on routine bloodwork — often the only early clue.
  • An enlarged liver (hepatomegaly) found by a doctor's examination or an imaging scan done for something else.
Easy to overlook: increased thirst · trouble sleeping or sleep apnoea · indigestion and bloating · "brain fog" or difficulty concentrating · reduced appetite · unexplained weight loss · mild itchiness.[2][3]
Why fatigue gets missed: obesity and type 2 diabetes — which usually appear before fatty liver — cause fatigue on their own. So increasing tiredness from the liver blends into tiredness you've already accepted as normal.[3]

Middle / progressing-stage signs

MASH with significant fibrosis · F2–F3

As inflammation and scarring advance, early symptoms intensify and new ones appear. Some people still have none — which is why staging requires tests, not feelings.

  • Worsening fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance.
  • Nausea, loss of appetite, and unintentional weight loss.
  • More constant right-upper-abdominal pain or a sensation of pressure.
  • Itchy skin (pruritus) that has no rash and doesn't respond to moisturiser.
  • Joint or muscle aches and general malaise.
  • Difficulty concentrating, memory lapses, mild confusion — early hints that the liver is clearing toxins less well.
  • Mild ankle swelling that appears by evening.
  • Worsening control of diabetes, cholesterol or blood pressure despite unchanged medication — a metabolic red flag clinicians watch for.
  • Darkening urine or paler-than-usual stools, even intermittently.
Act at this stage. F2–F3 is the window where treatment has the most to gain: scarring can still partially regress, and this is the fibrosis range in which approved MASH therapies are indicated.[12][13] It is also the range where a specialist referral changes your trajectory.

Late-stage signs

Cirrhosis & liver failure · F4 and beyond

These are signs of a liver that is failing and of portal hypertension. They are not subtle, and they are not something to monitor at home.[3][6]

  • Jaundice — yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes.
  • Ascites — a swollen, tense, fluid-filled abdomen that can develop quickly.
  • Oedema — swelling of the ankles, feet and legs.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy — confusion, disorientation, memory loss, personality change, daytime sleepiness with night-time wakefulness, a flapping hand tremor, and in severe cases hallucinations or coma.
  • Easy bruising and bleeding — nosebleeds, bleeding gums, prolonged bleeding from small cuts (the liver has stopped making clotting factors).
  • Spider angiomas — small spider-like blood vessels visible under the skin, usually above the waist.
  • Palmar erythema — reddened or blotchy palms.
  • Dark, tea-coloured urine and pale, clay-coloured or black tarry stools.
  • Severe, relentless itching over the whole body.
  • Muscle wasting — thinning arms and legs alongside a distended belly.
  • Vomiting blood, or black vomit that looks like coffee grounds — usually from ruptured oesophageal varices.
  • Hormonal changes — breast enlargement in men, testicular shrinkage, loss of libido, menstrual irregularity.
  • Enlarged spleen and low platelet count found on testing.
  • Shortness of breath, fever, and profound weakness.
Reality check: by the time these appear, damage is largely permanent. Everything above this point on the page exists so you can intervene before you get here.

Who is most likely to have it

Fatty liver tracks closely with metabolic health. Under the 2023 definition, a diagnosis requires liver fat plus at least one of five cardiometabolic criteria.[1]

The five cardiometabolic criteria (you need only one)

CriterionThreshold
Body weight / waistBMI ≥ 25 kg/m² — or ≥ 23 kg/m² for people of Asian descent — or waist circumference > 94 cm (men) / > 80 cm (women), or ethnicity-adjusted equivalent.
Blood sugarFasting glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L), or 2-hour post-load glucose ≥ 140 mg/dL, or HbA1c ≥ 5.7%, or diagnosed type 2 diabetes, or treatment for type 2 diabetes.
Blood pressure≥ 130/85 mmHg, or specific antihypertensive drug treatment.
Triglycerides≥ 150 mg/dL (1.70 mmol/L), or lipid-lowering treatment.
HDL cholesterol≤ 40 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L) in men, ≤ 50 mg/dL (1.3 mmol/L) in women, or lipid-lowering treatment.

Note the Asian BMI cutoff. Because Asian populations accumulate visceral fat at lower body weights, the threshold is 23 kg/m² rather than 25. Many Filipinos who consider themselves "not fat" meet this criterion.

🩸 Type 2 diabetes

The strongest driver. Roughly 55–70% of people with type 2 diabetes have fatty liver, and diabetes accelerates scarring.[14]

⚖️ Overweight & obesity

Incidence is about three times higher in people with overweight or obesity than at normal weight — especially with belly (visceral) fat.[14]

🥤 Metabolic syndrome

High triglycerides, low HDL and high blood pressure clustering together — the classic insulin-resistance pattern.

🧬 Genetics & family history

Variants such as PNPLA3, TM6SF2 and MBOAT7 raise susceptibility. A family history of cirrhosis is a recognised screening trigger.[9]

📅 Age & sex

Prevalence rises with age and is generally higher in men until menopause, after which women's risk climbs. Diagnosis is most common from the 40s onward — but not exclusively.

🧒 Children & teens

Paediatric prevalence is estimated at 5–10% and rising with childhood obesity.[15] Fatty liver is no longer an adult-only disease.

🍷 Alcohol — even moderate

Alcohol is not the cause of MASLD, but it accelerates damage. Regular intake above roughly 20 g/day (women) or 30 g/day (men) shifts the diagnosis toward MetALD and worsens outcomes.[1][12]

💊 Medicines & other conditions

Some drugs (e.g. amiodarone, tamoxifen, methotrexate, long-term steroids, valproate), hepatitis C, hypothyroidism, PCOS and sleep apnoea are associated with liver fat.

🌾 "Lean" fatty liver

Non-obese people can and do develop it. Studies in the Philippines put lean NAFLD at roughly 12–31% of cases.[16] Normal weight ≠ safe.

How doctors actually confirm it

Because symptoms are unreliable, diagnosis depends on tests. Here is the standard stepwise pathway used in current guidelines — useful to know before your appointment.

Step 1 — Blood tests

Liver function panel: ALT, AST, GGT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, albumin, and a platelet count.

Metabolic panel: fasting glucose or HbA1c, lipid profile, plus tests to exclude other causes — hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis C antibody, autoimmune markers, iron studies, thyroid function.

Step 2 — FIB-4 score

A free, simple calculation from your age, AST, ALT and platelet count that estimates scarring risk. It is the recommended first-line risk assessment.[9]

Roughly: < 1.3 suggests low risk of advanced fibrosis (re-check every 1–3 years); 1.3–2.67 is indeterminate and needs a second test; > 2.67 suggests advanced fibrosis and specialist referral. For people over 65, the lower cutoff is 2.0.[17]

Step 3 — Imaging

Abdominal ultrasound: the usual first look; detects moderate-to-severe fat but can miss mild cases and says nothing about scarring.

Elastography (FibroScan / VCTE): measures liver stiffness (fibrosis) and fat (CAP). A liver stiffness measurement of < 8 kPa helps rule out advanced fibrosis; > 12 kPa helps rule it in.[17] MRI-PDFF and MRE are the most accurate non-invasive options.

Step 4 — Liver biopsy

A small tissue sample examined under a microscope. Still the reference standard for confirming MASH and grading fibrosis F0–F4, but invasive and not needed for everyone.

Today, most staging is done non-invasively, and newer approved medicines can be started on the basis of imaging and blood tests rather than biopsy.[12]

Two traps that cause missed diagnoses

1. "My liver enzymes are normal, so my liver is fine." Not necessarily. Enzymes can stay within the reference range even while scarring advances, and some people have significant fibrosis with a completely normal ALT.[5] Normal bloodwork lowers suspicion; it does not remove it.

2. "An ultrasound said mild fatty liver, so nothing more is needed." Ultrasound detects fat, not scarring. Detecting fat is the start of the workup — the question that actually determines your prognosis is how much fibrosis is present, and that needs a FIB-4 score or elastography.

Treatment — and the genuinely good news

Caught early, fatty liver is one of the most reversible forms of organ damage there is.

Lifestyle remains the foundation

  • Weight loss of 5–10% of body weight is the single most effective intervention — it reduces liver fat, resolves inflammation and can improve fibrosis. Even 3–5% helps reduce fat.[9]
  • 150–250 minutes of moderate exercise per week (brisk walking, cycling, swimming) — plus resistance training. Exercise reduces liver fat even without weight loss.[17]
  • Mediterranean-style eating: vegetables, whole grains, legumes, fish, nuts, olive oil. Cut added sugar, sugar-sweetened drinks and fructose — these are among the strongest dietary drivers of liver fat.
  • Avoid alcohol, or keep it minimal. There is no known "safe" amount once scarring has begun.
  • Control the co-existing conditions: blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol. Statins are safe in fatty liver and are not a reason to avoid treatment.[9]
  • Coffee — regular consumption is associated with lower liver fat and fibrosis in observational studies. Not a treatment, but not something to avoid.
  • Vaccinate against hepatitis A and hepatitis B if you're not immune, so a second liver insult doesn't stack on the first.
A serious caution about supplements: do not self-prescribe "liver detox" teas, cleanses, high-dose vitamins or herbal capsules. Herbal and dietary supplements are a well-documented and growing cause of drug-induced liver injury, and they are frequently unregulated. Vitamin E at prescription doses has a role in selected non-diabetic adults with biopsy-proven MASH, but guidelines do not support taking it routinely — the long-term risk/benefit balance is unsettled.[17] Ask your doctor before starting anything.

Medicines approved for MASH (as of 2025–2026)

For decades there was no approved drug. That has changed — but these are prescription medicines for a specific stage, decided by a specialist, never something to seek out on your own.

Resmetirom (Rezdiffra)

The first FDA-approved MASH therapy (March 2024) — an oral, liver-directed thyroid hormone receptor-beta agonist. Indicated for adults with non-cirrhotic MASH and moderate-to-advanced fibrosis (F2–F3), alongside diet and exercise. Not recommended in decompensated cirrhosis.[12]

Semaglutide (Wegovy 2.4 mg)

The first GLP-1 receptor agonist approved for MASH (August 2025, accelerated approval based on the ESSENCE trial), also for non-cirrhotic MASH with F2–F3 fibrosis. In the trial, steatohepatitis resolved in 62.9% vs 34.3% on placebo.[13]

Also used: pioglitazone and GLP-1 agonists in people who also have type 2 diabetes; careful management of blood pressure, lipids and cardiovascular risk. Not recommended as a liver treatment: metformin (no histological benefit) and ursodeoxycholic acid.[17]

Availability varies widely by country. Neither drug is necessarily approved, registered, reimbursed or affordable in every health system. This is a question for your own physician or pharmacist, not something this page can answer for you.

If cirrhosis has already developed

Management shifts to preventing complications: low-sodium diet and diuretics for ascites, medication and endoscopic banding for oesophageal varices, lactulose or rifaximin for encephalopathy, ultrasound surveillance for liver cancer every 6 months, screening for portal hypertension, nutrition support, absolute alcohol abstinence, and liver transplant evaluation in decompensated disease.[17]

Eight myths that keep people from getting checked

Each of these is a real reason people delay — and each is wrong.

✕"I'm thin, so I can't have fatty liver."
✓Lean and non-obese fatty liver is well documented — roughly 12–31% of cases in Philippine studies, and around 11.7% of non-obese people in pooled South-East Asian data.[16] Visceral belly fat and insulin resistance matter more than the number on the scale.
✕"My blood test was normal."
✓Normal ALT does not exclude disease. Some people have significant fibrosis with normal liver enzymes.[5]
✕"I don't drink, so my liver is safe."
✓Alcohol is only one cause. Fatty liver is now driven mainly by metabolic factors — which is exactly why the name changed in 2023.[1]
✕"I feel fine, so there's nothing wrong."
✓Fatty liver has been called a silent disease for good reason. Symptoms typically arrive at MASH or cirrhosis — years after damage began.[2]
✕"It's just fat. It's harmless."
✓It is the most common chronic liver disease worldwide, the fastest-growing cause of liver cancer, and it roughly 1.5×–2.5× your cardiovascular risk.[11][18] People with MASLD live about three years less on average.[4]
✕"Once you have it, it's permanent."
✓Early-stage disease is often fully reversible with weight loss and metabolic control. Long-term studies show improvement in a meaningful proportion of patients.[7]
✕"It only happens to older adults."

✕"A liver cleanse or detox tea will fix it."

If you are reading this from the Philippines

Some context specific to the local situation — the risk factors here are moving in the wrong direction.

Local data worth knowing

  • There is still no national prevalence figure for fatty liver disease in the Philippines. Hospital-based studies estimate a range of roughly 10–19.9%, while some community screening has found substantially higher rates — one review notes around one-third of the general population.[16] The honest answer is: we don't know the true national number yet.
  • A study at the Philippine General Hospital found NAFLD in 12.2% of liver-disease patients; a later multi-centre Liver Databank study found 19.31%, making it the second most common liver disease in the country after hepatitis B — with cases rising about 30% between 2010 and 2017.[16]
  • Diabetes is extremely common among Filipino fatty-liver patients — reported in 47–69% of cases, well above the global pooled figure of about 22.5%.[16]
  • The DOST-FNRI 2025 Updating Survey found obesity among Filipino adults aged 20–59 had risen from 39.8% (2023) to 44.5% (2025) — nearly one in two adults — and higher among women (50.1%), urban residents (48.3%) and the wealthiest households (57.8%).[19] Obesity is a direct driver of fatty liver.
  • The survey uses the Asia-Pacific BMI classification, where ≥ 25 kg/m² counts as obese — a lower bar than Western cutoffs, because Asian bodies carry more metabolic risk at the same weight.
  • Hepatitis B is endemic here (~10%). That matters: a person can have fatty liver and viral hepatitis at the same time, and the two together damage the liver faster than either alone. It also means a "fatty liver" finding on ultrasound should never be assumed to be the whole story without proper blood tests.
  • Liver diseases accounted for about 27.3 per 1,000 deaths in the Philippines, and liver cirrhosis prevalence is estimated at 328.4 per 100,000 for compensated disease.[16]

Where to start locally: a barangay health centre or any general physician can order the basic bloodwork (liver enzymes, fasting glucose or HbA1c, lipid profile) and an abdominal ultrasound. From there, a gastroenterologist or hepatologist handles staging and specialist treatment. PhilHealth coverage for specific tests and medicines changes over time — confirm current coverage with your facility or with PhilHealth directly. For a medical emergency, call 911.

Please get checked — and here's how to make the visit count

You cannot diagnose fatty liver from a webpage, an app, a symptom list or a home test. Here's what to bring instead.

Consider booking an appointment if…

  • You have type 2 diabetes, prediabetes, obesity, high blood pressure or high cholesterol — screening for advanced fibrosis is recommended in these groups.[9]
  • A previous ultrasound or blood test mentioned "fatty liver," "hepatic steatosis" or raised ALT/AST and you never followed up.
  • You have ongoing fatigue or a persistent ache under your right ribs.
  • There is a family history of cirrhosis, liver cancer or fatty liver.
  • You drink alcohol regularly and also carry metabolic risk factors.
  • You are over 40 with a growing waistline and have never had liver enzymes checked.

Bring these to your appointment

  • All previous results — bloodwork, ultrasound reports, CT/MRI, anything mentioning your liver. Trends matter more than single numbers.
  • A complete list of everything you take: prescription drugs, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, herbal products, teas and supplements. This is essential — some of these affect the liver.
  • An honest alcohol history — how many drinks, how often, over how long. Doctors can only stage correctly if this is accurate; there is nothing to be embarrassed about.
  • Your weight history and any recent unintentional weight change.
  • Your symptom timeline — what you've noticed, when it started, what makes it better or worse.

Questions worth asking your doctor

  1. Do I have fat in my liver — and if so, how much scarring (fibrosis) is there? That's the question that determines my risk.
  2. Can we calculate my FIB-4 score from the blood tests you've already ordered?
  3. Should I have elastography (FibroScan) or another staging test, rather than ultrasound alone?
  4. Do I need screening for hepatitis B and hepatitis C as well?
  5. How much weight do I need to lose, and what is a realistic timeline?
  6. Are any of my current medicines or supplements hard on my liver?
  7. Do I need a referral to a gastroenterologist or hepatologist?
  8. How often should I repeat testing — and what result would change the plan?
  9. Am I a candidate for any approved MASH medication, and is it available and covered here?
  10. Should I be screened for liver cancer?

Why we keep repeating this

This page was written to help you recognise a silent, common and largely reversible condition early — and to push you toward a real consultation, not to replace one.

Health information online is general by nature. Your liver is not. Two people with identical symptoms can have completely different diagnoses; two people with the same diagnosis can have completely different fibrosis stages and completely different prognoses. The symptoms listed above are shared with gallbladder disease, hepatitis, ulcer, kidney problems, heart failure, thyroid disease, anaemia, depression and dozens more. Only a clinician who examines you, reviews your history and orders the right tests can sort that out.

If something on this page made you uneasy about your own health — treat that as a reason to book an appointment, not as a diagnosis. And if you have any of the emergency warning signs, stop reading and seek care now.

Frequently asked questions

Can fatty liver be cured completely?

In the early stages, often yes. Simple steatosis and early MASH can resolve with sustained weight loss of 5–10%, dietary change, physical activity and control of diabetes and lipids. Once cirrhosis (F4) is established, the scarring is largely permanent — though progression can usually be slowed or stopped, and some fibrosis can regress with effective treatment. This is precisely why timing matters so much.

How fast does it get worse?

On average, fibrosis advances about one stage every 14 years in simple MASLD and about every 7 years once MASH is present.[4] That's an average — roughly one in five people progress considerably faster, especially with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes, ongoing alcohol use or obesity.[7] Progression to cirrhosis typically takes years to decades.

Is NAFLD the same thing as MASLD?

Yes — for the overwhelming majority of patients they describe the same disease. The name changed by international consensus in June 2023.[1] The diagnostic criteria are slightly different (MASLD requires at least one positive metabolic risk factor, rather than merely excluding alcohol), but an older "NAFLD" or "NASH" report remains medically valid. NASH is now MASH.

Will I need a liver transplant?

Most people never will. Transplant becomes a consideration only when disease progresses to decompensated cirrhosis — typically signalled by jaundice, ascites, variceal bleeding or hepatic encephalopathy.[3] Fatty liver disease has nonetheless become one of the leading indications for liver transplantation in many countries, which is why early detection is emphasised so heavily.

What's the difference between this and alcoholic liver disease?

Both cause fat, inflammation and scarring, and both can end in cirrhosis. The difference is the primary driver: alcohol intake versus metabolic dysfunction. Fatty-liver symptoms tend to be milder, slower and vaguer, developing over years or decades; alcohol-related liver disease often produces jaundice and abdominal fluid more rapidly, particularly with heavy daily intake.[2] The two frequently overlap — that combination is now classified as MetALD, and it carries a worse outlook than either alone.

Can children get fatty liver?

Yes. Paediatric prevalence is estimated at 5–10% and is rising with childhood obesity.[15] Global pooled data suggest roughly 9% in boys and 6.3% in girls, with far higher rates among children with obesity. There is no national paediatric prevalence data for the Philippines yet.[16] If your child has obesity, type 2 diabetes or abnormal liver enzymes, raise it with a paediatrician.

Does having fatty liver mean I'll get liver cancer?

No — the absolute risk for most people is low, but it is meaningfully higher than average and rises steeply with fibrosis stage. MASLD is the fastest-growing cause of hepatocellular carcinoma worldwide.[11] In large pooled analyses, people with MASLD and advanced fibrosis had roughly an 8–11× higher liver cancer risk than those without advanced fibrosis. Concerningly, a substantial share of fatty-liver-related liver cancers occur in people who never developed cirrhosis — so staging and specialist follow-up matter.

Should everyone get screened?

Current AASLD guidance advises against screening the general population, but recommends risk assessment for high-risk groups — people with type 2 diabetes, medically complicated obesity, a family history of cirrhosis, or more than mild alcohol consumption — and FIB-4-based risk assessment for anyone with detected liver fat or suspected MASLD.[9] European guidance similarly recommends case-finding in people with type 2 diabetes, or abdominal obesity plus another metabolic risk factor, or persistently raised liver enzymes.[17] Discuss your individual risk with your doctor.
